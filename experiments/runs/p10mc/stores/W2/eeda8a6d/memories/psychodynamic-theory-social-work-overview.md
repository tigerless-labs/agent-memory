## Psychodynamic Theory: Hierarchical Overview

**Psychodynamic Theory (Definition & Scope)**
- Originally derived from Sigmund Freud (early 1900s), now a network of theories
- Also known as psychoanalytic psychotherapy
- Core idea: forces outside conscious awareness explain behavior; roots in childhood experiences

**Historical Evolution: Four Schools of Thought**

1. **Drive Theory (Freud)**
   - Human behavior results from drives: eros (sex, self-preservation), death (destruction/aggression)
   - The Id, Ego, Superego model
     - Id: primitive, instinctual part containing drives
     - Superego: moral conscience, societal morals learned from parents
     - Ego: mediates between id and external world; handles decision-making
   - Defense mechanisms activate when drive-based desires trigger conflict

2. **Ego Psychology**
   - Ego does more than regulate drives; develops autonomously
   - People have innate drive to explore and control their environment
   - Both environment and reality matter to development

3. **Object Relations Theory**
   - Behavior shaped by relationships with significant others (parents, siblings)
   - Adults repeat object relationships formed in childhood
   - Focus: maintaining relationships while differentiating self

4. **Self Psychology**
   - Self-perception defined by boundaries/differences from others
   - Healthy narcissism has a place in development
   - Lack of self-esteem/sense of self linked to parental empathy deficits in childhood
   - Self-regulation and resilience stem from healthy sense of self

**Key Assumptions of Psychodynamic Theory**
- All behavior has underlying cause
- Causes originate in the unconscious
- Different unconscious aspects struggle against each other
- Adult behavior/mental health rooted in childhood experiences
- Both internal/innate processes and external environment shape adult personality

**Goals of Psychodynamic Therapy**
- Clients acknowledge emotions and recognize patterns
- Identify patterns in behavior and relationships
- Improve interpersonal relationships
- Recognize and address avoidance behaviors with healthy coping mechanisms

**Psychodynamic vs. Other Therapies**
- Psychodynamic: explores deeply rooted drives, needs, desires (global approach, emotions-focused)
- Modern therapies (e.g., CBT): mitigate/eliminate symptoms (problem-based, behavior-focused)

**Diagnostic Frameworks**
- DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual) — standard in psychology/psychiatry
- PDM (Psychodynamic Diagnostic Manual, published 2006, revised 2016) — alternative/supplementary guide
  - Sponsored by major psychoanalytic associations

**Treatment Techniques in Psychodynamic Social Work**
- Talk therapy (primary)
- **Transference**: client redirects feelings/expectations from one person onto clinician (usually unconscious); helps clinician understand client's life
- **Free association**: client speaks/writes all thoughts as they come; reveals connections between topics
- **Dream analysis**: understanding unconscious through dreams; used in person-centered, CBT, and Gestalt therapy

**Application to Social Work**
- Social workers balance multiple client issues (trauma, mental health, unemployment, discrimination, etc.)
- Psychodynamic theory provides framework for understanding seemingly unrelated symptoms
- Fits well with social work's focus on interpersonal relationships (client-worker relationship as tool for understanding and change)
- Allows clients to reach root of thoughts/emotions/behaviors; promotes self-discovery

**Strengths of Psychodynamic Theory**
- Recognizes impact of childhood on adult mental health
- Accounts for both nature (internal drives) and nurture (childhood experiences)
- Led to psychoanalysis and the therapeutic tool of talking/conversation
- Modern evidence supports benefits of psychodynamic therapy

**Criticisms of Psychodynamic Theory**
- Too deterministic; lacks personal agency; victim-blaming
- Freud's original theories: sexist (women inferior, weaker superegos, prone to anxiety)
- Historical bias: Freud mostly treated middle-aged women in Vienna
- Modern criticism: insufficient/inadequate for people of color and LGBTQ+ communities
- Originally ignored social issues: sexism, racism, poverty, homophobia
- Overemphasis on psychotherapy vs. addressing broader social systems
- Concerns for social workers: tension with social justice values; need to consider social structures affecting clients

**Scientific Status**
- Criticized as unscientific; difficult to prove; largely unsubstantiated
- Modern medical advances provide supporting evidence for psychodynamic therapy benefits
