## Key figure: Gerbert of Aurillac (946–1003)

Later became Pope Sylvester II. Instrumental in popularizing mathematical and scientific study in medieval Europe.

- Studied extensively from non-Christian sources (Arabic, Greek manuscripts)
- Visited Muselmen libraries in Iberian peninsula; learned Roman surveying, abacus, Arab numerals
- Wrote *De Geometria*, compilation of Euclidean knowledge
- Set new standard: studying different sources, thinking for oneself, testing knowledge through discussion and correspondence
- Rose to imperial court tutor (Otto III) and eventually papal office despite accusations of heresy

## Medieval mathematics education: the quadrivium

Four subjects valued for mystical and spiritual aspects rather than proofs:

- **Arithmetica**: counting boards, Roman numerals, ratios, biblical numerology, computus
- **Musica**: chord theory connected to string-length ratios, notation of notes
- **Geometria**: geometric objects (triangles, cones, spheres, regular solids); equilateral triangle associated with Holy Trinity; proofs not emphasized but objects valued for connection to world order
- **Astronomia**: movement of stars and planets (Ptolemaic system); sometimes conflated with astrology

Mathematical knowledge sought to help "human mind rise to reach spiritual truths" and to recognize God's creation patterns in nature.

## Rise of universities (universitas)

First cathedral schools founded at wealthy monasteries; became centers of learning with special privileges:

- **Bologna** (1088)
- **Sorbonne/Paris** (1150)  
- **Oxford** (1164)

Structure: groups of scholars (mostly monks) debating among themselves and teaching pupils. Granted exemptions from taxes, weapon-carrying rights, own courts and internal laws. Developed as distinct subcultures within medieval society.

Within universities, mathematical fields could develop in directions different from Christian mysticism practiced outside their walls.
