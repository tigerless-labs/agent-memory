Purpose: Establish objective criteria for determining whether a property appraisal order is complex, to justify higher fee requests and guide consistent team assessment.

Five-Category Complexity Checklist:

**Location**: Remote area/limited access; unique environmental features (hillside, water, wooded); high crime/environmental hazards; challenging street layout (cul-de-sac, steep driveway).

**Property Type and Design**: Unusual/complex design (multi-unit, multiple levels, attached garage); special features (pool, hot tub, large deck); unusual HVAC (geothermal, radiant); outbuildings (barn, shed, guesthouse).

**Age and Condition**: Significantly older/newer than area typical; requires extensive repairs/renovations; significant wear/tear (water damage, pest infestation); major alterations (single-family to multi-unit conversion).

**Zoning and Land Use**: Unique zoning/restrictions (floodplain, conservation, historic); complex land use regulations (HOA rules, wetlands); zoning variances or conditional use permits.

**Market Conditions**: Rapidly appreciating/declining market; unique characteristics (vacation homes, high-end concentration); unusual trends (unemployment, foreclosures); supply/demand challenges (limited properties, specific feature demand).

Key Principle: An appraiser's opinion that a property is complex does not automatically establish its complexity. Appraisers have financial incentives to claim complexity for higher fees, so the team must rigorously evaluate assertions against objective criteria and independent analysis.
