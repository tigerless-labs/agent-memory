Key works exploring how word meanings are understood and communicated across different schools of linguistic thought.

## Saussure: Course in General Linguistics
Core concepts:
- **Arbitrary nature of sign** — no inherent connection between a word and what it refers to
- **Signifier vs Signified** — the word form (signifier) represents a mental concept (signified)
- **Langue vs Parole** — langue is the abstract language system; parole is actual language use
- **Synchronic analysis** — study language at a single moment rather than tracing historical change
- **Structural analysis** — a word's meaning comes from its relationships with other words in a sentence

## Ogden & Richards: The Meaning of Meaning (1923)
Theory of meaning based on word-referent relationships:
- **Symbol vs Signal** — a symbol (like a word) has arbitrary, conventional meaning; a signal has direct relationship to action
- **Connotation matters** — meaning includes cultural associations and feelings, not just the object referred to
- **Contextual definition** — the same word means different things in different contexts (e.g., "line" in "line on a map" vs "line in a play")

## Chomsky: Syntactic Structures (1957)
Generative grammar theory:
- Language structure is innate to the human mind; children learn language through activation, not imitation
- **Universal grammar** — all languages share basic structural rules (e.g., subject-verb-object patterns)
- **Language acquisition device** — humans have an inborn capacity for rapid, effortless language learning
- **Deep structure vs Surface structure** — underlying grammatical meaning (deep) differs from actual words used (surface); different sentences can share the same deep structure
- Meaning determined by sentence structure: same words in different arrangements mean different things

## Lakoff & Johnson: Metaphors We Live By (1972)
Core insight: metaphorical language shapes thought and understanding:
- Language and thought are interconnected; how we speak reflects how we think
- Conceptual metaphors are pervasive and reveal how humans understand abstract concepts
- Examples:
  - *Argument is war* — we use combat language ("attacked my position," "defended my argument")
  - *Time is money* — we treat time as a valuable commodity ("waste time," "spend hours")
  - *Love is a journey* — relationships are framed as trips ("rough patch," "been through a lot")
  - *Ideas are food* — intellectual content gets culinary descriptions ("meaty argument," "hard to swallow")

## Research Context
Spans from foundational semiotics (Saussure) through structural linguistics (Ogden & Richards, Bloomfield) to generative grammar (Chomsky) to cognitive linguistics (Lakoff & Johnson). Each school offers different answers to how meaning is formed, transmitted, and understood.
